Project Framework & Academic Analysis

About the Project

This website serves as a platform meant to inform about the heavy decline of African local languages, and as an advocacy tool for the revival of the use and status of of african languages.

1. The proof of death

How do we actually know they’re dying out, though? Well, according to the UNESCO data, nearly 40% of the estimated 2000 to 2500 indigenous languages spoken across Africa are all experiencing different levels of endangerment. But what does that actually mean? What scale can we use for this? Well, linguistics tend to use the Graded Intergenerational Disruption Scale (GIDS). Now, why is it a great scale? Well, it shows us that language death happens from an early stage; it starts from the restricted use of languages in public spaces, to the workplace and schools. All these factors tie into it and it is usually on 8 stages.

Now one might wonder, “is it really Africa? Or just some specific part, like the Southern region.” Well, it is true that it’s not the whole of Africa, but each one of the five regions is experiencing this.
At least 40 local Ghanaian languages face extinction. There’s a drop in fluency in the local tongue amongst local Nigerian youth compared to other rural youth. Minority languages such as Yaaku and El Molo in Kenya are either critically endangered or extinct by UNESCO standards. There’s 0 transmission of Yaaku amongst the youth. The N|uu language only has one fluent speaker from South Africa

And then one tends to ask, “Okay, the situation is real, but how bad is it. Well, according to a research article, if a language usage tends to show the following pyramid structure, then it’s pretty bad.
The Eldest Demographic (Ages 65+): Hold absolute, fluent command of the indigenous language, including deep mastery of complex proverbs, historical oral narratives, and specialized ecological vocabulary.
The Middle Demographic (Ages 35–64): Possess conversational fluency but frequently rely on code-switching (mixing local words with English or French) due to the demands of their professional lives.
The Youth Demographic (Ages 15–34): Displays a sharp decline in usage. They use the language strictly for informal, highly simplified phrases, or they abandon it entirely in favor of the official state language.
The Child Demographic (Ages 0–14): Grow up entirely monolingual in English, French, or a dominant regional tongue, suffering a total break in ancestral language transmission.

And the pyramid above sounds a bit too familiar to most African communities. Wonder why?

Today, this systemic linguistic hierarchy is maintained by educational institutions and policymakers who continue to exclude foundational languages from formal learning curricula and professional spaces (Brenzinger, 1992). Because of this ongoing institutional neglect, newer generations are forced to rely almost entirely on foreign languages for academic validation and social mobility (Abrams and Strogatz, 2003). This structural setup creates severe generational friction within communities (Lingu.Africa, 2025). When families drop their native tongues at home to ensure their children's economic survival, it forms an acute language barrier between youth and older relatives (Okyere, 2024). This gap disrupts the traditional oral transmission of folklore, history, and ancestral philosophies, leaving the younger generation culturally dispossessed and permanently severed from their roots (Honyiglo, 2025).

2. The Effect: What happens when a language dies out?

Linguistic research establishes that a language is not merely a interchangeable set of vocabulary words or basic grammar rules; it is an entirely distinct cognitive lens and a cultural repository. When a language dies, a community suffers a severe structural loss across three critical axes:

Epistemological Loss

Language carries knowledge. It carries stories, wisdom, ways of living, and so much more. Language is an intricate part of culture. When a language dies, the whole library of ecological knowledge dies out as well. Unique terminology cannot simply be translated into English, French, or Kiswahili. When the languages die out. Any medicinal knowledge is lost as well. They aren’t just random sounds that are unimportant. They are tombs to a world of knowledge threatening to be lost for ever if not preserved.

Gap in Community and Loss of Identity

“Eraka lyafu, vantu vafu,” a saying from the Kwangali people of the Kavango, in Namibia, which directly translates to “The language has died, the people have died.” Language is a lens through which people's identities are formed. Ask any African if they’re the same person when they speak a different language; they’ll most likely tell you no. From the rhythm of speech, to the everyday poems, the language a person knows shapes the way they even think. So when a mother tongue is lost, the way of life of the community it represented would also be lost. Imagine a Zulu who did not know the meaning of Ubuntu. And not just that, the shift in fluency rates acroos generations, also serves as a barrier between generations. Young peopel feel alienated from their culture, and a sense of loss of identity.

Generational Fracturing

The rapid shift toward official languages creates an acute communication barrier within families (Okyere, 2024). When younger generations grow up speaking only English or French, and older relatives primarily speak their mother tongue, the chain of oral transmission breaks down. Elders can no longer effectively pass down oral histories, complex family genealogies, moral frameworks, and traditional folklore (Honyiglo, 2025). This linguistic disconnect isolates the youth from their heritage, leaving them culturally dispossessed and severed from their family roots.
