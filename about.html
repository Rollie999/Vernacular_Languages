Project Framework & Academic Analysis

Intro To Analysis

This page of the website hopes to answer three main questions that one might have when considering the whole issue of language death across the continent. These three questions help in actually analysing the issue at hand here, and more subtly offer up the solutions.

1. Question 1: How do we know languages are actually dying out?

Well, according to tracking data published by UNESCO, nearly 40% of the estimated 2000 to 2500 indigenous languages spoken across Africa are all experiencing different levels of endangerment. But what does that actually mean? What scale can we use for this? Well, linguists tend to use the Graded Intergenerational Disruption Scale (GIDS). Now, why is it a great scale? Well, it shows us that language death happens from an early stage; it starts from the restricted use of languages in public spaces, to the workplace and schools. All these factors tie into it, and it is usually on 8 stages.

Now one might wonder, “Is it really Africa? Or just some specific part, like the Southern region.” Well, it is true that it’s not the whole of Africa, but each one of the five regions is experiencing this.

  • As recorded in national educational reports published by Graphic Online, at least 40 local Ghanaian languages face extinction.
  • There’s a drop in fluency in the local tongue amongst local Nigerian youth compared to other rural youth.
  • Minority languages such as Yaaku and El Molo in Kenya are either critically endangered or extinct by UNESCO standards. There’s 0 transmission of Yaaku amongst the youth.
  • The N|uu language only has one fluent speaker from South Africa.


And then one tends to ask, “Okay, the situation is real, but how bad is it." Well, as analyzed in mathematical and social shift models by researchers Abrams and Strogatz, if a language usage tends to show the following pyramid structure, then it’s pretty bad:
  • The Eldest Demographic (Ages 65+): Hold absolute, fluent command of the indigenous language, including deep mastery of complex proverbs, historical oral narratives, and specialized ecological vocabulary, as documented in local linguistic audits published by Graphic Online.
  • The Middle Demographic (Ages 35–64): Possess conversational fluency but frequently rely on code-switching (mixing local words with English or French) due to the demands of their professional lives.
  • The Youth Demographic (Ages 15–34): Displays a sharp decline in usage. They use the language strictly for informal, highly simplified phrases, or they abandon it entirely in favor of the official state language.
  • The Child Demographic (Ages 0–14): Grow up entirely monolingual in English, French, or a dominant regional tongue, suffering a total break in ancestral language transmission, according to data from Right for Education.


And the pyramid above sounds a bit too familiar to most African communities. Wonder why?

Question 2: How Did We Get Here?

Now that we’ve shown there’s proof in the decline in languages, let’s actually analyse how we got here. The issue, one could argue, started from an event that has left many challenges for the continent: colonisation. As documented in historical linguistic studies edited by Matthias Brenzinger, European colonial administrations imposed the initial sidelining of African languages. To centralise administrative power, colonial legal political structures established foreign tongues - English, French, and Portuguese- as the language of governance, law, and economic wealth. Native languages were denied official status and labelled as primitive “vernaculars” or dialects unfit for intellectual thought. As recorded in educational reports published by Graphic Online, this hierarchy was enforced violently; students were often punished for speaking their mother tongues within school walls. This historic enforcement conditioned generations to view their native tongues as obstacles to social status and intellectual advancement.

Now, you’d think the whole ordeal would be solved by governments once countries started gaining independence. Well, it is a bit trickier than that. According to tracking data published by UNESCO, Africa currently has 54 countries, but at least 2000 languages. A math student would tell you by the pigeonhole principle that one country had at least 38 languages spoken in it. Now setting it back to the post-colonial error. A new nation has just gained independence, so what language does it choose to use for government businesses when there are at least 38 to choose from, and how does the government choose one so as not to make the others feel oppressed or sidelined? Hence, the most obvious choice is the colonial language. People were already learning it in schools, and it avoided a civil war entirely. Now, although this choice was politically convenient for the new government, as analyzed in mathematical and social shift models by researchers Abrams and Strogatz, it kept the colonial language at the top of the hierarchy; local languages remained locked out of service systems, continuing their marginalisation.

Okay, fast forward to the 21st century, and you'd think the internet and modern world would give these languages a fresh canvas. Well, it’s the exact same story playing out on a different stage. This structural exclusion has expanded straight into our technological and environmental landscapes, rendering indigenous languages increasingly unusable in the modern world. Look at how technofocal domination plays out. The rapid growth of the digital economy acts as a massive modern force driving language decline. Over 95% of digital interfaces, operating systems, mobile applications, and software tools utilised across the continent are built exclusively in global European languages, something that derivation.co would easily show. Local minority languages lack the essential digital infrastructure—such as standardised digital alphabets, localised keyboard layouts, and dedicated Natural Language Processing datasets—needed to even exist online. Because international tech corporations rarely invest capital into engineering software for low-resource regional languages, younger generations find their native tongues completely unusable across internet-based platforms, forcing them to adopt dominant languages for digital survival.

And it doesn't stop online; environmental and ecological displacement push it even further. As global climate change and rapid urbanization alter local landscapes, rural communities face severe displacement. As recorded in ecological tracking papers by Right for Education, because traditional ecological knowledge, specialised naming systems for native flora, and localised agricultural wisdom are woven directly into the syntax of indigenous languages, the physical destruction of these environments, coupled with moving to urban centers where global languages dominate, completely severs the context in which these languages naturally thrive.

2. The Effect: What happens when a language dies out?

Linguistic research establishes that a language is not merely an interchangeable set of vocabulary words or basic grammar rules; it is an entirely distinct cognitive lens and a cultural repository. When a language dies, a community suffers a severe structural loss across three critical axes:

Epistemological Loss

Language carries knowledge. It carries stories, wisdom, ways of living, and so much more. Language is an intricate part of culture. When a language dies, the whole library of ecological knowledge dies out as well. Unique terminology cannot simply be translated into English, French, or Kiswahili. When the languages die out. Any medicinal knowledge is lost as well. They aren’t just random sounds that are unimportant. They are tombs to a world of knowledge threatening to be lost for ever if not preserved.

Gap in Community and Loss of Identity

“Eraka lyafu, vantu vafu,” a saying from the Kwangali people of the Kavango, in Namibia, which directly translates to “The language has died, the people have died.” Language is a lens through which people's identities are formed. Ask any African if they’re the same person when they speak a different language; they’ll most likely tell you no. From the rhythm of speech, to the everyday poems, the language a person knows shapes the way they even think. So when a mother tongue is lost, the way of life of the community it represented would also be lost. Imagine a Zulu who did not know the meaning of Ubuntu. And not just that, the shift in fluency rates acroos generations, also serves as a barrier between generations. Young peopel feel alienated from their culture, and a sense of loss of identity.

Generational Fracturing

The rapid shift toward official languages creates an acute communication barrier within families (Okyere, 2024). When younger generations grow up speaking only English or French, and older relatives primarily speak their mother tongue, the chain of oral transmission breaks down. Elders can no longer effectively pass down oral histories, complex family genealogies, moral frameworks, and traditional folklore (Honyiglo, 2025). This linguistic disconnect isolates the youth from their heritage, leaving them culturally dispossessed and severed from their family roots.
